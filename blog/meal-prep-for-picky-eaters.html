Meal prep for picky eaters

Published 12 November 2026 · 7 min read

Planning a week of dinners is one problem. Planning a week of dinners when one person at the table will reject anything with a sauce, anything green, and anything where the foods touch is a different problem — and most meal-prep advice quietly pretends it does not exist.

The usual answer is to cook two dinners: the real one, and the plain pasta. That works, which is why so many parents end up doing it. It is also the single biggest reason meal planning collapses in a household with a fussy eater, because you have doubled the thing you were trying to make smaller.

There is a better structure, and it is not a trick to get your child to eat broccoli. It is a way of building dinner so that one pan feeds everyone without negotiation.

1. Stop cooking meals. Cook components.

This is the whole thing, so it goes first.

A finished, mixed dinner — a casserole, a curry, a stir-fry already tossed together — is a single take-it-or-leave-it object. A picky eater can only accept or reject the whole. And once they reject it, you are cooking again at 6:40pm.

The same ingredients served as separate components become something else entirely. Rice here, shredded chicken there, the sauce in a bowl, the vegetables in a third dish, the cheese and tortillas on the table. The adults build a burrito bowl. The eight-year-old eats rice, plain chicken and three pieces of cheese. Nobody cooked twice. Nobody negotiated.

This is why the prep method we teach is one protein, one grain, one vegetable rather than five finished meals in containers. We arrived at it because finished meals do not survive a changed plan — but it turns out to be the picky-eater answer too, for the same underlying reason. Components can be recombined. Casseroles cannot.

Deconstructing the plate is not a compromise. For most of the dinners a family already cooks, it is just a different serving order.

2. Put one guaranteed food on the table every night

Pick something cheap, boring and always available: bread and butter, plain rice, a bowl of cut fruit, a tub of yoghurt. It goes on the table every single night, as part of the meal rather than as a reward or a replacement.

Two things happen. The child knows there is something there they can eat, which takes the fear out of a new dish and makes them more likely to try it. And you stop being the backup kitchen, because the backup is already on the table and you did not have to cook it.

The rule that matters: the guaranteed food is not a bargaining chip. It is not withheld, and it does not have to be earned by eating three bites of something else. The moment it becomes leverage, dinner becomes a negotiation again, which is the thing we are trying to delete.

3. Plan five dinners, and let one of them be theirs

We argue everywhere on this site that you should plan five dinners a week rather than seven, because a seven-night plan needs a seven-night week and you do not have one. In a picky-eater household there is a second reason.

Give one of the week's nights to your kids — genuinely, with a say in what appears. A child who chose Thursday is a different person at the table on Thursday. It is also one fewer decision you have to make, and the option they pick is almost always something you already know how to cook.

Expect the chosen meal to be beige. That is fine. One beige night, chosen by the person eating it, buys goodwill for the other four.

4. Serve new food next to known food, in small amounts

If a dinner contains something unfamiliar, the plate should still be mostly familiar. A spoonful of the new thing beside a portion of something known reads as optional. A plate that is half-covered in it reads as a demand, and it gets pushed away before it is tasted.

Serving it and not commenting is usually more effective than any amount of encouragement. Children are extremely good at detecting that a food matters to you, and a food that matters to you is a food worth refusing.

5. Repeat things on purpose

The instinct with a fussy eater is to keep searching for the dish that finally works. It is the most tiring possible approach and it has the worst odds, because every new dinner is a fresh chance to be rejected.

Familiarity is what does the work. A food often needs to show up on the table many times before a cautious child will try it, and that only happens if you are willing to serve the same things repeatedly and let most of those occasions go nowhere.

Write down the dinners your household already eats without a fight. In a picky-eater home that list feels humiliatingly short from the inside — and it is still usually ten to fifteen meals long when you actually count. Ten reliable dinners on rotation is a solved problem, not a boring one. Variety is a preference adults have on behalf of children who did not ask for it.

What this looks like across a week

Concretely, from one Sunday prep block — roast and shred chicken, cook a big pot of rice, wash and cut peppers and cucumber:

  • Monday — burrito bowls. Rice, chicken, beans, cheese, peppers, all in separate bowls. The picky plate is rice, chicken and cheese.
  • Tuesday — quesadillas with cut cucumber on the side. Almost universally accepted; essentially a cheese toastie.
  • Wednesday — pasta, with the sauce served from a jug rather than stirred through. Buttered pasta and parmesan is a complete plate for whoever wants it.
  • Thursday — the kids' pick.
  • Friday — pizza, topped individually.

Two nights are left blank on purpose, as always. Every one of those dinners is one cooking job, and every one of them can be eaten in a plain version by someone who wants a plain version.

One honest caveat

Everything above is about ordinary fussiness — the child who eats eleven things and regards the twelfth with suspicion. That is common and it generally loosens with time and repetition.

It is not the same as a child whose eating is genuinely restricted: a very short list of accepted foods that is still shrinking, distress or gagging at the table, or a worry about weight and growth. That is worth raising with your doctor or a paediatric dietitian rather than solving with a planner. We write printable planning pages, and we are not going to pretend a planner is the answer to that.

If you only change one thing

Serve the next dinner you were going to mix together in separate bowls instead, and put bread on the table. You have not cooked anything extra and you have not asked anyone to try anything. See what gets eaten.
