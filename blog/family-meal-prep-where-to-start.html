Family meal prep: where to start when you have no time

Published 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Most meal-prep advice is written for someone with a free Sunday afternoon, a second fridge and a tolerance for eating the same lunch five days running. If that is not your life, here is a version that fits into one hour and does not require you to become a different person.

Start by naming the actual problem

Before buying containers, be precise about what is going wrong, because the fixes are completely different:

  • “I don’t know what to cook.” A deciding problem. Fixed by a fixed list and a posted plan.
  • “I know what to cook but there’s no time at 6pm.” A prep problem. Fixed by component prep.
  • “We have food but it isn’t the right food.” A shopping problem. Fixed by an aisle-sorted list written against a plan.
  • “We throw a lot away.” An inventory problem. Fixed by writing down what is in the fridge and freezer.

For most parents of school-age kids, the first one is the biggest and it is almost never the one people try to solve. They buy a slow cooker, when what is exhausting them is making the same decision twenty times a week while someone small asks about it every ten minutes.

The first hour, in four blocks

Minutes 0–10: pick five dinners

Five, not seven. Choose from meals you already cook well. If you cannot think of five, write down everything you have cooked in the last month first — the list will be longer than you expect, and it is reusable forever.

Leave two nights blank on purpose. Those two blanks are not laziness, they are the shock absorber that keeps the plan alive when Wednesday goes sideways.

Minutes 10–25: write one list, by aisle

Go dinner by dinner and write every ingredient under a store section: produce, meat and fish, dairy and eggs, pantry, frozen, other. Stand in the kitchen while you do it and check the cupboards as you write. One trip, no duplicate tins, no Wednesday dash for garlic.

Minutes 25–30: put the plan on the fridge

Not in a notes app. On paper, at eye height, where every member of the household can read it. This is the step people skip and it is the one that removes the most mental load, because the plan starts answering the question instead of you.

Minutes 30–60: prep three components

After shopping, prep exactly three things and then stop:

  1. One protein. Roast and shred chicken, brown a big batch of mince, or bake a tray of sausages.
  2. One grain. A large pot of rice, pasta, or boiled potatoes. Cool it quickly and cover it.
  3. One vegetable. Washed and cut, stored where you can see it. Vegetables in a drawer do not get eaten.

Those three turn into tacos, burrito bowls, fried rice, a tray bake, a soup or a stir-fry. Nothing is committed to a specific night, which is exactly why it survives contact with a real week.

Four things that make the second week easier than the first

Week one of anything is powered by enthusiasm. Week two is powered by how little thinking it requires.

  • Repeat on purpose. Cook the same five dinners next week if they worked. Rotation is the goal, not a failure of imagination.
  • Cook double once a week. Brown twice the mince on taco night and Thursday is already done. This is the cheapest trick in family cooking.
  • Give one night away. Let a child choose it, within two or three options you can live with. Fewer arguments, one less decision for you.
  • Write down what goes in the freezer. Including the date. Unlabelled containers become archaeology within a month.
An unfinished hour still beats no plan. Set a timer, stop when it goes off, and use whatever you managed.

What not to buy yet

Not a chest freezer, not a vacuum sealer, not thirty matching containers, not a subscription. Those are optimisations for a system that is already running. The only things the first month needs are a printed page, a pen, and a fridge magnet.
